The regulatory landscape for clinical AI translation, comparing LATAM, US, and EU postures in 2026. This is the post I wish someone had written when I started.

**United States.** HIPAA at 45 CFR Parts 160 and 164 is the foundational framework. The 2024 OCR clarifications added explicit guidance on AI-mediated PHI processing: the AI vendor is a Business Associate, the model is a tool not a separate actor, the audit obligations apply to AI-generated outputs the same as to human-generated outputs. The Joint Commission's 2024 language-access standards tightened documentary evidence requirements. The FDA's SaMD (Software as a Medical Device) framework applies if the system makes clinical decisions; pure translation systems typically fall outside SaMD but the boundary is testable on a case-by-case basis. Net posture: stringent, well-documented, expensive to comply with, but a clear path.

**European Union.** GDPR at Articles 5, 6, 9, and 32 governs PHI processing. Article 9 explicitly classifies health data as a special category requiring explicit consent or one of a small set of legal bases. The EU AI Act, in force since August 2024, classifies medical interpretation AI as a high-risk system under Annex III, requiring conformity assessment, post-market monitoring, and detailed technical documentation. The new dimension since 2024 is the AI Liability Directive, which shifts the burden of proof in damage claims toward the AI provider. Net posture: stringent, evolving fast, market access is gated by formal compliance.

**Peru.** Law 29733 on Personal Data Protection, enacted 2011, regulation Decreto Supremo 003-2013-JUS. The Authority for Personal Data Protection is the regulator. Health data is sensitive under Article 2 and requires explicit consent for processing, with limited exceptions for healthcare delivery. SUSALUD regulates private healthcare. DIGEMID regulates pharmaceuticals. MINSA sets clinical standards. Net posture: clear framework, accessible regulator, lighter on documentary burden than HIPAA or GDPR, but increasing convergence toward international standards.

**Colombia.** Habeas Data Law 1581 of 2012 plus Decreto 1377 of 2013 implement personal data protection. Supersalud regulates healthcare. The regulator is the SIC (Superintendencia de Industria y Comercio). Net posture: similar to Peru in approachability, distinct enough in specifics that a single LATAM compliance program needs country-by-country tuning.

**Mexico.** LFPDPPP (Ley Federal de Protección de Datos Personales en Posesión de los Particulares) of 2010 plus its regulations. COFEPRIS regulates health-related products and services. The regulator is INAI. The CNBV regulates financial services. Net posture: extensive but uneven enforcement, expanding actively in 2025-2026.

**Brazil.** LGPD (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados) of 2018 modeled closely on GDPR. ANPD is the regulator. ANVISA regulates health. Net posture: most rigorous in LATAM, closest in stringency to GDPR, mature enforcement record from 2022 onward.

The right operating thesis for a clinical AI company is multi-jurisdictional compliance from day one. Trying to be HIPAA-only and add LGPD later costs more than building both in parallel. The compliance work compounds — most of the controls (encryption, audit logs, access controls, breach notification, data subject rights) apply across all five frameworks. The framework-specific work is the residual 20 percent.

Orquor's compliance roadmap (in `/07-legal/` and the migration runbook) is built around this thesis. We did not choose between regulatory frameworks. We built the architecture that satisfies all of them at the floor.
