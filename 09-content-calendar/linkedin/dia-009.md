What does a single mistranslated dose actually cost a hospital?

The 2016 ECRI Institute analysis of language-related adverse events estimated direct settlement costs of language-mediated medical errors in US hospitals at USD 350,000 to USD 4.2 million per case, before plaintiff legal fees and regulatory penalties. That number excluded reputation damage, accreditation complications with The Joint Commission, and the operational disruption of an internal investigation that typically runs 6 to 14 months.

The numbers are higher in 2026. Punitive damages have crept up. The Joint Commission's tightening of language-access standards in 2024 raised the bar for documentary evidence — meaning a hospital that cannot produce a defensible audit trail of how the patient was communicated with is in a structurally weaker position before discovery has even begun.

A common objection: "we use a human interpreter, the documentation is implicit in our nursing notes."

That objection survives until the plaintiff's lawyer asks for the recording. There is no recording. The interpreter cannot remember the exact wording six months later. The nursing note says "interpreter present". The defense rests on a memory.

Compare that posture to a cryptographically timestamped audit log that records the exact ASR transcript, the exact translation delivered, the exact verifier results, and the exact register applied — with a signature chain that an external auditor can verify against Bitcoin blockchain anchors.

The difference is not technological. It is forensic. And the cost of not having it is set by the largest case that goes to trial, not by the average case that settles quietly.

Every hospital that scales clinical AI in 2026 will end up with one of two postures: defensible by mathematics, or defensible by recollection. Choose now.
