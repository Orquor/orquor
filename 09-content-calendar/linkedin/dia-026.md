Why we built Orquor in Lima instead of San Francisco. The answer is not about cost.

The standard narrative about LATAM tech is that you build here because engineering salaries are lower and the timezone overlaps with the US. Both are true. Both are table stakes. Neither is why we made this decision.

We made it because the Spanish-speaking healthcare market does not have a technology center of gravity. It has demand — 62 million Spanish speakers in the US healthcare system, 480 million across LATAM — and it has no company building clinical AI infrastructure specifically for Spanish-first clinical workflows. The market is there. The infrastructure is not.

Lima sits at the geographic and linguistic center of LATAM Spanish. Clinical Spanish in Lima carries the vocabulary, register, and idiomatic patterns that map cleanly to Bogotá, Mexico City, San José, Miami, and Buenos Aires. A model trained on Lima-sourced clinical data generalizes across the LATAM clinical Spanish space. A model trained on the linguistic edge — Chilean clinical Spanish, Argentine clinical Spanish — does not generalize as cleanly. Lima is not the cheapest place to build. It is the neutral place to build, and neutrality matters when your training data has to represent 20 countries.

The talent argument is also real. PUCP, UTEC, UNI, UPC — the engineering pipeline in Lima produces graduates who have shipped at Meta, Anthropic, Stripe, Google, Cloudflare. Many of them want to return. The diaspora-to-reverse-migration pipeline exists. It is not a theory; it is our current engineering team.

The regulatory argument is the least discussed and the most important. Peru's data protection authority (Autoridad Nacional de Protección de Datos Personales) is approachable. A founder can request a technical briefing and receive a response in weeks, not months. The regulator is interested in the technology, not hostile to it. Building in a jurisdiction where the regulator will talk to you at design time is worth more than any amount of post-deployment defensibility.

The sovereign AI argument closes the case. In the last 18 months, Peru, Colombia, Mexico, and Brazil have each launched domestic AI infrastructure programs. Innóvate Perú, ProInnovate, BID Lab, the Brazilian PROFLOR. These are not large programs by US or EU standards. But they represent a policy direction: Latin American governments are deciding that sovereign AI capability matters. A company positioned at the intersection of healthcare AI and LATAM sovereignty is in the right place at the right time.

We are not building in Lima because it is cheaper. We are building in Lima because it is the right place for the problem we are solving.
