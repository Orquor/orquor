How we calibrated the verifier weights in the Orquor Clinical suite. Not the theory. The method.

The verifier suite ships with weights from 1 to 5 across 60 verifiers. A weight of 5 means a failure blocks the translation and escalates to the human interpreter. A weight of 1 means a failure is logged for the post-session report but does not interrupt the session. The difference between 5 and 1 is the difference between a blocked translation and a note in a report. Getting the weights wrong means blocking translations that should have passed, or passing translations that should have been blocked.

The calibration method we used:

**Step 1 — Clinical expert panel.** Three clinicians — an emergency physician, a clinical pharmacist, and a certified medical interpreter with 12 years of ER experience — independently reviewed 500 annotated translation pairs containing deliberate errors across 12 failure modes. They rated each error on a 5-point clinical consequence scale. Inter-rater agreement was 0.81 (Cohen's kappa), which is strong but not perfect. The disagreements were not random; they clustered around the boundary between weight 3 and weight 4.

**Step 2 — Anchoring to adverse event data.** We cross-referenced the expert ratings against published adverse event data from the Joint Commission's sentinel event database (2019–2024) and the IMIA's adverse event taxonomy. Dosage magnitude errors appear in 38% of language-barrier adverse events in the Joint Commission dataset. Drug-name substitution errors appear in 22%. These two categories alone account for 60% of language-barrier adverse events. Every verifier that detects an error in either category was assigned weight 5, with no exceptions.

**Step 3 — Boundary-case adjudication.** The disagreements between the expert panel (the 0.19 not captured by kappa) were adjudicated by a fourth reviewer — a healthcare attorney with clinical risk management experience. The attorney's mandate was not to resolve clinical disagreements but to adjudicate the legal exposure: if this error reached a patient without detection, would it constitute a standard-of-care violation? The attorney's adjudications shifted three verifiers from weight 3 to weight 4 and two from weight 4 to weight 5.

**Step 4 — Prospective validation.** After calibration, we ran the weighted suite against a held-out set of 200 translation pairs the panel had not seen. The suite blocked 94% of catastrophic errors (weight 5 violations), 78% of moderate errors (weight 3), and 45% of stylistic errors (weight 1). The false-positive block rate — translations blocked that the clinicians later judged should have passed — was 2.1%.

The calibration is not static. Every quarter, we re-run steps 2 and 4 against the accumulated corpus of production translations and verified adverse events. Weights can change. When they do, the version increments and the audit log records the cutover.

Calibrating verifier weights is not a machine-learning problem. It is a clinical risk management problem. The right methodology is expert panel + epidemiological anchoring + legal adjudication + prospective validation. Every clinical AI company shipping verifier-based systems should publish their calibration methodology. Ours is above.
