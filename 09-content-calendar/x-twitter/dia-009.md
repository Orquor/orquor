**Format**: 6-tweet strategic-reasoning thread. **Hook**: a question that sounds counterintuitive.

**1/**
We published the ACTO specification openly. No patent. No trademark on the term. Several advisors thought it was a mistake.

Here is the reasoning behind the decision. The frame may be useful to others making similar choices.

**2/**
Patent: gives you a defensible moat in marketing for 3 years on this category. After that, the patent erodes — competitors route around it, the prior art accumulates, the enforcement cost rises.

We do not want a 3-year marketing moat. We want a 10-year category position.

**3/**
Trademark on the category term (ACTO): forces every competitor to use a different word. They will. The word they pick becomes the standard term. We are left holding a trademark on the unused word.

Coca-Cola owns "Coke" because they popularized it. Naming it does not transfer ownership; using it does.

**4/**
Open specification: the category becomes the standard. Every competitor explains themselves in the vocabulary we coined. We become the implementation that defined the category.

The category position outlasts any patent. The implementation position is what gets acquired or scales.

**5/**
The risk of openness is being copied. The risk is real. The mitigation is implementation quality and dataset accumulation, neither of which a specification reveals.

A competitor reading the whitepaper learns what to build. They do not learn how we built it well. The two are different.

**6/**
The deeper bet is this: ACTO is a category that should not be owned by a single vendor. Healthcare is too important. Building it in public is partly a strategic move and partly a duty.

The vendors who later compete in this category will not have been able to define it. We will have. That position is durable.
